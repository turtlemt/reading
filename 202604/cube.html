Introduction

A Rubik’s Cube looks simple. It has six sides, six colors, and a few small pieces. Yet it can be arranged in about 43 quintillion different ways. Today, some people can solve it in less than three seconds. How did this colorful puzzle become a world sport?

The Origin

In 1974, Ernő Rubik, a professor of architecture in Budapest, Hungary, built a wooden cube whose parts could turn without falling apart. He wanted to help his students understand three-dimensional shapes. After he twisted the cube, he could not put the colors back in order. It took him about a month to solve his own puzzle.

Rubik called his invention the “Magic Cube,” and it went on sale in Hungary in 1977. In 1980, a toy company renamed it the Rubik’s Cube and sold it around the world. More than 450 million have been sold since then, which makes Rubik one of the most successful toy inventors in history.

From Toy to Sport

The first World Championship was held in Budapest on June 5, 1982, with players from 19 countries. The winner solved the cube in 22.95 seconds. Later, interest slowed down for a while, but the internet brought it back. Fans shared videos, learned new methods, and found one another online. In 2004, the World Cube Association (WCA) was founded to write fair rules and keep official records. Today, the WCA also runs events such as one-handed solving, blindfolded solving, and bigger cubes.

Competition Today

The sport is bigger than ever. In 2024, there were more than 2,700 WCA competitions in 102 countries. They had over 63,000 participants, including nearly 33,000 newcomers. The 2025 World Championship in Seattle welcomed about 2,000 competitors from 74 countries. That was the biggest championship in WCA history, compared with 1,187 competitors in 2023.

World Records

As of October 2026, the world record for a single solve is 2.51 seconds. It was set by Xuanyi Geng of China at the Guangzhou Grand Open 2026. The record for an average of five solves is 3.51 seconds, set by another Chinese cuber, Yiheng Wang. The average is harder to beat because one slow solve can ruin the whole attempt. Compared with 22.95 seconds in 1982, the best solve today is more than nine times faster. Better methods, smooth magnetic cubes, and endless practice all helped.

Conclusion

The Rubik’s Cube began as a tool for a classroom and grew into a sport for people of all ages. Even if you never break a record, it can train your patience, memory, and logical thinking. And records do not last forever. Geng was only ten years old when he first set the single record in 2025.
